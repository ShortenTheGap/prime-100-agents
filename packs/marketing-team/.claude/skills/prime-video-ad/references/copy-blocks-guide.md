## 1. Pain/Problem

The prospect's primary motivation for watching: they want help with a specific pain or problem they're currently experiencing. The more impactful or urgent the problem, the more motivated the prospect will be — which makes it easier to create and intensify the itch.

**Key principle: Target what prospects are experiencing NOW.** "Prevention" of a possible future problem sells much less than solving a problem they're living with right now. After establishing the current pain, you can intensify stakes by talking about what happens if they fail to solve it (an urgency motivator).

### Problem Tokens

Don't refer to the problem in boring, vague terms. Use "problem tokens" — describe the symptoms the way prospects experience them in daily life.

- Core problem: "passionless relationship" → Problem token: "he kisses you like you're his Aunt"
- Core problem: "loneliness/lack of friends" → Problem token: "eating alone at a restaurant again"
- Core problem: "stubborn belly fat" → Problem token: "not being able to cross your legs because you don't want your boyfriend to see your muffin top"

Problem tokens are vivid, specific, and slightly uncomfortable. That discomfort is the point.

## 2. Promise

The foundation of direct response marketing: specific promises that solve the prospect's urgent problems. Effective promises imply a clear, specific, desirable benefit that directly impacts the prospect's life in a meaningful way.

**Key principle: Promises should move the prospect from their current identity to their desired identity.** From who they are now to who they want to be and how they want to be viewed by others.

Examples:
- "Lose 30 pounds of stubborn fat" (weight loss)
- "Sign 3 new retainer clients in the next 30 days" (copywriters)
- "Deliver a best man speech that gets a standing ovation" (wedding speeches)

**Edge of believability:** Effective promises sound almost "too good to be true" but have just enough plausibility that prospects don't dismiss them. They want to believe the result is possible — and we want them to click through to find out HOW it's possible.

This doesn't mean making absurd claims. It means painting clear pictures of a desired, even ideal, outcome.

## 3. Curiosity

All great ads overflow with curiosity elements. Curiosity is the primary driver that stops prospects from scrolling, captures their attention, and buys a window of opportunity.

### Category 1: Hidden Cause / Unique Mechanism of the Problem

The idea that there's a lesser-known or newly discovered cause behind the prospect's widely accepted problem. This gives prospects hope that you can actually help, even though others have failed.

Examples:
- "Leaky Joint Syndrome" as the hidden cause of joint pain
- "Toxic Backpack" to explain difficulty losing weight through dieting
- "Melatonin Drain" to explain trouble sleeping

### Category 2: Unique Mechanism of the Solution

A new, unique, exciting discovery that serves as the "vehicle" for delivering the promise. Instead of overexplaining (which is boring and creates resistance), tease the solution in compelling, curiosity-inducing ways.

The way you tease the solution is always based in what the solution actually is, but it's OK to caricature it — focus on the interesting, enticing, novel aspects without telling the full story.

Examples:
- "7-second salt trick that flushes away belly fat" (instead of "a supplement that contains sodium")
- "The Tiny MICRO STOCK set to create new millionaires"
- "'Speakeasy Email' trick for writing world-class emails with 30-second voice notes"

**Amplification through origin:** Curiosity elements get stronger when you leverage where the cause/solution/discovery came from, especially if it's strange, exotic, or unexpected.

Instead of "a berry rich in antioxidants," say "an incredible berry found on the coastline of Okinawa that stimulates your body's natural GLP-1 production… which is why scientists from Japan are calling it the Ozempic Berry."

### Category 3: Visual Curiosity (Video-Specific)

Since ads display in video form, leverage visual curiosity as a pattern interrupt. Visual curiosity doesn't need to follow tight logic — it diverts expectations of what prospects would typically see in an ad. This is especially important in the opening frames.

Examples:
- Instead of talking about losing weight, show what 10 pounds of fat looks like strapped to someone's body
- Instead of showing a supplement bottle, show an ambiguous dessert dish that "melts away pounds" — make it look desirable to eat but don't clearly explain ingredients
- A personal trainer hanging upside down from a pull-up bar, talking about how most people think you need crazy workouts to get results

## 4. Proof

When teaser ads target the correct Pain/Promises and introduce compelling curiosity elements, prospects want to believe. Proof gives them permission to believe.

### Credible Proof

A credible expert or institution backing up the claims.
- "A 2024 Harvard study shows..."
- "Dr. Sarah Chen, leading researcher at..."

### Social Proof

Relatable or aspirational figures already using the teased solution to achieve the desired results.
- "Jan, a 42-year-old mom from Ohio, lost 37 pounds."

### UGC (User Generated Content) — Video-Specific

For social media video ads, the most effective proof comes directly from users. Short testimonial snippets that fulfill one or more copy blocks — just the highlights, quick high-impact soundbites chained together throughout the ad.

## 5. Constraints

Perceived limitations that prospects feel will prevent them from solving the problem. Whether factual or not, these constraints are 100% real in the prospect's mind. If you don't address them, prospects won't be able to envision success.

### Five Types of Constraints

**Identity Constraints:** Who do prospects see themselves as? What do they see themselves as capable of? If their crystallized self-image conflicts with what you're promising, it requires finesse.

**Values Constraints:** Two components — (1) prospects won't take action on things that conflict with their personal values/principles, and (2) does your product deliver the outcome they actually want?

**Belief Constraints:** Limited beliefs about their inability to solve the problem. Both internal (beliefs about themselves) and external (beliefs about solutions/the market/the problem).

**Resource Constraints:** What does the prospect feel they're lacking that's required to solve the problem? Time, energy, money, experience, reputation, skills.

**Experience Constraints:** How have past experiences restricted their capability of envisioning a future where they've solved the problem? ("Clients always ghost me," "I always cheat on my diet")

### How to Dismiss Constraints in Ads

Great teaser ads dismiss constraints by implying prospects can achieve results despite their limitations:

- Conditional statements: "WITHOUT having to count calories... spend hours in the gym... or eat restrictive rabbit food diets"
- Despite statements: "EVEN IF you're already working a full-time job and only have an hour a day"
- Proof through others: Show testimonials from people who had the same constraints yet still succeeded — this is the most effective and efficient approach
- Visual dismissal: Show the constraint being eliminated visually rather than always stating it explicitly

## Combining Blocks

The most effective lines hit multiple blocks simultaneously:

- "Jan, a 42-year-old mom from Ohio, lost 37 pounds" = Proof + Promise + Constraint elimination (age, being a busy mom)
- "Harvard scientists are in shock..." = Credible Proof + Curiosity amplification
- "Without medications, surgeries, or hours of exhausting exercise" = Constraint elimination x3

The best ads choose the most compelling copy block for the opening hook, then rapidly stack blocks with high velocity throughout.

---

## Copy Velocity

90–95% of the ad's content should establish one or more copy blocks. Ruthlessly limit words that aren't directly related to a block. This is interruption marketing — nobody planned on watching the ad. Once you've grabbed attention, each line must intensify curiosity and desire.

Worry less about the ad making perfect logical or conversational sense. The ad should flow, but what you're really doing is stacking high-impact statements related to the various copy blocks. That's what creates the most emotional impact.

The only other copy permitted is dimensionalizing emotional rewards — future pacing prospects or letting them envision how the teased solution will specifically impact their life (the social or emotional benefits of achieving the desired outcome).
