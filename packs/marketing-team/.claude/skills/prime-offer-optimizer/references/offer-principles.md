# Offer principles

The doctrine behind the Prime Offer Optimizer. Read it before Stage 1 and again before Stage 2. No em dashes, no en dashes, anywhere in this skill or its output.

## The eight elements

Every offer review considers all eight. Two of them are optional (guarantee and bonuses) and are left out when they do not earn their place.

### 1. Person

Who is this built for?

A usable Person has three parts: the relevant problem, the ability to pay for the solution, and the conditions needed to benefit from it. The third part is the one owners skip. A buyer who cannot give the process what it needs will not get the result no matter how good the offer is.

- Use the Living Avatar when it exists. It is the only description of the buyer written in the buyer's own words.
- A buyer is not made specific by adding irrelevant demographic detail. Age, city, and job title matter only when they change the problem, the ability to pay, or the ability to benefit.
- "Anyone who wants more customers" is not a Person. Neither is a list of five different buyers.

### 2. Promise

What changes for the buyer?

- Use a concrete outcome, in the buyer's terms, not yours.
- Separate outcomes from deliverables. A number of calls, videos, templates, or hours is what the buyer receives. The promise is what those things let them do or stop doing.
- Include a timeframe only when it is supplied by the owner, supported by evidence, or clearly labeled as proposed. Never attach a timeframe because it makes the promise sound stronger.
- One promise per offer. Competing promises mean nothing was chosen.

### 3. Process

How does the work lead to the result?

- Explain the main steps and how each one connects to the outcome.
- Separate the process from the delivery format. Calls, videos, dashboards, portals, and communities are formats. None of them explains how a result happens.
- Where it helps the owner decide, explain the business payoff of a step. Never treat hours saved as cash saved or as revenue.
- A process that could describe any competitor is not a process yet. Say what remains unclear rather than hiding it behind a name.

See `process-explanation.md` for the exercise that produces this.

### 4. Proof

Why should the buyer believe the promise?

- Use relevant case studies, demonstrations, measured results, or other real evidence.
- Prefer proof that matches this buyer and this promise. A result from a different kind of customer is weaker proof than the owner thinks it is.
- Use the MOAT proof map when it exists. It already pairs evidence to the beliefs that stop people buying.
- Keep the context: customer type, starting point, result, timeframe, limits.
- If proof is weak or missing, say so plainly and suggest a practical way to collect some. A debrief with the last three clients usually produces more than a year of waiting for a perfect case study.
- One exceptional result is never presented as a typical outcome.

### 5. Guarantee

What risk does the business take off the buyer?

Optional. Never create one just to fill the template, and never create one the owner cannot afford to honor.

If there is one, it names all five of these:

1. The promised result or experience.
2. The measurement period.
3. The client's reasonable responsibilities.
4. The remedy.
5. The relevant limits.

Conditions exist to support client success and must be easy to understand. Never design a condition whose real job is to block a legitimate claim. Always evaluate what the guarantee costs to honor, and put that number in the economics run.

### 6. Payment

What does the buyer pay, and when?

- Include the price, the payment schedule, the engagement period, and any other charges you know about.
- Never invent a price.
- You may propose pricing options. Mark them as proposals, show the reasoning, and list the assumptions they rest on. A proposal is a business change, not a copy edit.

### 7. Path to pay

What is the next step?

- One clear primary action that fits how the owner actually sells: apply, book a call, buy a pilot, or buy directly.
- Never invent a live URL or a payment link. Use a visible placeholder and say what it needs.
- If the owner sells by conversation, the action is the conversation. If nobody is on the other end, the action cannot be "reply".

### 8. Bonuses

What useful extra removes an obstacle?

Optional. Include one only if it helps the buyer succeed, reduces their effort, or removes a real concern they have named.

- No unrelated items.
- No inflated dollar values.
- No extra homework dressed up as a gift. A bonus that adds work to the buyer's week makes the offer worse.

## Offer Mindset Shifts

How the owner should think about their offer. Call these **Offer Mindset Shifts**. Never call them beliefs: MOAT uses "belief stack" for the buyer's false beliefs, and the two must not be confused.

Use a shift only when it is relevant to the decision in front of the owner. Add the context only when it helps them decide. These are teaching principles, not universal laws.

1. Old: "If people buy, my offer is strong." New: "If strangers have a clear reason to buy, my offer is strong." Context: referrals and reputation may be doing most of the selling. Appeal to strangers still has to be tested in the real world.
2. Old: "More services make my offer more valuable." New: "A more specific result makes my offer more valuable." Context: extras help only when they support a result the buyer values.
3. Old: "A rich buyer is a good buyer." New: "A rich buyer with an urgent problem is a good buyer." Context: ability to pay and motivation to act are two different things.
4. Old: "Businesses making money are good buyers." New: "Businesses that can afford the help they need are good buyers." Context: revenue alone does not prove buyer fit or good delivery economics.
5. Old: "A clever name makes my process different." New: "A clear explanation makes my process different." Context: the explanation has to describe a real difference. A name alone never does.
6. Old: "A good offer is one that sells." New: "A good offer sells and stays profitable to deliver."
7. Old: "I need exceptional people to grow." New: "I need a repeatable process my people can deliver." Context: strong people still matter. The goal is to avoid needless dependence on rare expertise.
8. Old: "I should lead with what clients need." New: "I should lead with what clients want and deliver what helps them succeed." Context: the initial promise still gets fulfilled. This is not permission for a bait and switch.
9. Old: "Done for you means doing everything." New: "Done for you can mean doing one valuable part."
10. Old: "A stronger offer needs bigger claims." New: "A stronger offer needs stronger reasons to believe." Context: relevant proof and a credible process often matter more than another claim or another guarantee.

## Market selection

These factors only make sense together, never one at a time:

- Number of reachable buyers.
- Ability to pay.
- Urgency.
- Difficulty of acquiring buyers.
- Buyer willingness to implement.
- Required delivery effort.
- Repeat purchase or retention potential.

Two broad options worth teaching:

**Larger market, lower price.** Usually needs more buyers, low delivery cost per buyer, repeatable delivery, a workable way to acquire customers, and buyers willing to do enough of the work themselves.

**Smaller market, higher price.** Usually needs buyers with enough money, a problem worth real money to them, credible proof, enough support or implementation to get the result, and economics that carry both acquisition and delivery.

**The dangerous mismatch:** a small pool of buyers who cannot pay much but need a lot of hands on help. Flag it the moment you see it.

Rules when you talk about markets:

- Do not imply that every mid priced offer is bad, or that these two are the only viable models.
- Never invent market size figures. If the owner needs one, say what would have to be counted.
- Separate a buyer seeking relief from an urgent problem from a buyer seeking an optional improvement. They buy differently.
- A successful, wealthy buyer can still have an urgent problem in one specific area. Wealth does not remove urgency.
- Enterprise sales, account based marketing, and strategies for landing large corporations are out of scope for this skill.
