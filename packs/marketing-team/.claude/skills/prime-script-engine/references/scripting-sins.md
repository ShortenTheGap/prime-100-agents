# Scripting Sins - Anti-Patterns Reference (Prime)

Source: the July 6 and July 13 live coaching calls. Devin compiled these from reviewing every member video submission: the three biggest mistakes in scripting, two of which happen before a word of the script is written. Format: mistake, diagnosis, fix. Use this as the audit layer: a script that follows every commandment can still die from any sin below.

**The three-sin frame.** Sin 1 is choosing the wrong idea (fix: narrow problems). Sin 2 is choosing the wrong solution for the idea (fix: Trinity of Value). Sin 3 is presenting it in a way that won't perform (fix: kill shtick talk in the hook). They cascade: a narrow problem makes a Trinity-passing solution easy to find, and a solid solution leaves only presentation to fix. Underneath all three: the idea is the PROBLEM you solve; the direction is the SOLUTION you give. Most people conflate them.

---

## Sin 1: The idea (what problem you pick)

**1. Solving a broad problem.**
Diagnosis: an impossible problem can't be solved in 90 seconds, so you're forced into vague, surface-level advice. The viewer leaves with homework and a thousand new questions instead of a win, and gets negatively reinforced against your next video. Broad advice speaks to everyone and resonates with no one. Tells: the viewer can't recall your steps seconds later; "here's how to lose 100 pounds" answered with "calorie deficit, protein, lift weights."
Fix: narrow the problem until you can solve it COMPLETELY: zero questions, zero homework, a guaranteed small win. Smart creators guarantee small wins; dumb creators attack big problems. Evidence: full leg workout, 2K views, vs Jeff Nippard's seat-back leg extension, 7.2M. "How to land a job" vs the resume video: 100x views, and the narrow video was LONGER. Jeff's calf-raise range-of-motion video: his most viewed ever, 23M.

**2. Niching when you mean to narrow.**
Diagnosis: niching shrinks WHO the video is for ("grow your legs if you have bad knees" throws away every ideal client with good knees). Narrowing shrinks WHAT you solve while staying relevant to the whole avatar ("best seat position for leg extensions" still covers everyone).
Fix: narrow the problem, never the audience. Stopwatch rule: the moment the next step down would exclude noticeable percentages of your ideal clients ("for tall lifters"), you've hit fully narrowed. Stop there.

**3. Narrowing past the viewer's awareness.**
Diagnosis: the statement of the call: narrow until you can guarantee a win based on your ideal viewer's ASSUMED level of knowledge. Two failure directions: mapping out steps they already know (a Trial Reels video can assume they can post on Instagram), or going so deep you'd have to teach them the thing exists (a front-raise variation nobody has heard of can't be the problem you solve).
Fix: define the avatar's assumed knowledge first (the relevant worksheet / war plan), then narrow to one problem they know they have, sitting just past what they already know.

**4. Listing steps instead of choosing problems.**
Diagnosis: when narrowing, members drift into "step one, step two" of solving the big problem (when to do the family introduction, what to say first). That's sequencing a solution, not selecting a problem. If the viewer isn't aware they have it, it's not the video's problem.
Fix: at every level ask "what narrower problem, that the viewer knows they have, could this video solve completely?" Sibling problems at the same depth (seating arrangements, conversation starters, what not to say) are separate videos, not steps of one.

**4b. The heterogeneous listicle (a broad video in a trench coat).**
Diagnosis: a "5 things" video feels narrow because it has a count, but if the items are different KINDS of thing (a file, a concept, an integration, a strategy principle), each item opens a new mental model and the video raises more questions than it answers. Item count is not narrowness.
Fix: the cheat-code test. Every item in a list must be the same noun-type, the same action-verb, and roughly the same effort, with an immediate payoff (install a plugin, ask a question, delete a setting). Grade the all-encompassing test PER ITEM, not per video. And run the trench-coat test on your own list: if two or more items deserve their own video, the list is a broad video in disguise. (Evidence: the 6.4x plugins reel worked because all five items were install-and-done; a "5 setup concepts" version of the same video fails this test.)

**5. Copying big accounts' ordinary videos.**
Diagnosis: authority is real (the same video from a nobody won't do 23M views), but a big creator's average video proves nothing. A 1M-view Jeff Nippard video is an UNDERperformer for him.
Fix: only learn from outliers. Establish the account's average, then model only videos far above it. The market decides what's good; the outlier flag is how you read its answer. And don't stall on "I'm not the authority yet": are you going to stop posting? No. So don't focus on it.

## Sin 2: The solution (what you give for the problem)

**6. Telling WHAT without HOW.**
Diagnosis: no clear solution. The video compares A to B instead of bridging them ("I build systems that trigger DMs daily"). If the viewer didn't know how before, they still don't. Inspiration at best.
Fix: the video is a simple, all-encompassing path from A to B. Exit test: could a viewer act with no extra research and no unanswered steps?

**7. Making a reminder, not a video.**
Diagnosis: not unique. Clear and correct, but the avatar already holds this solution ("bulking meals have more calories," chicken and rice). "Your video is no longer valuable, it's just a reminder."
Fix: be easier, more effective, or better explained than the solution already in the viewer's mind. A new analogy that finally makes the form click counts as unique.

**8. Entertaining while calling it education.**
Diagnosis: not viewer depictable. Clear, wildly unique, and nobody will ever do it (jailbroken iPads, half a pair of Jordans, backyard herb tinctures). Save-worthy, not do-able.
Fix: run the three depictability checks against the avatar: can they see themselves doing it, do they trust it works, is it worth the effort? If you're deliberately making entertainment, fine, but know which game you're playing, and consider the hybrid: entertaining process, then "you don't have to make this, I sell it."

**9. Discarding solutions because they add steps.**
Diagnosis: members reject solutions that aren't easier. Unique doesn't require easier: the multivariant hook adds five steps.
Fix: judge efficiency, not ease. Slightly harder for significantly more payoff still reads as unique and valuable ("a little bit harder for significantly more payoff").

**10. Calling an expertise problem a content problem.**
Diagnosis: "I can't find differentiating ideas" usually means shallow explanations, not exhausted ideas. Jenna's case: the fix wasn't new topics but a better lateral-raise analogy proven on real clients. Hormozi had 100 unique objection handles because he did 4,000 sales calls first.
Fix: mine lived experience. Build explanations and analogies that work on real clients; you become a better coach and get better videos from the same act.

## Sin 3: The presentation (the hook)

**11. Shtick talk: stealing hooks that hit without knowing why.**
Diagnosis: every member raised a hand to "stole a winning hook and it flopped." The stolen words were not the reason it worked: the bubble-butt squat hook worked because of who said it; the prison money video worked because a prisoner said it; the sung "steal these viral hooks" did 7M while Devin's spoken trial reel of the same words did 3,000.
Fix: before using any borrowed hook, name why it hit. Then make it yours with one of three moves: unique presentation (who says it, how it's said, how it's shown: 7'2" Cole doing "how to squat for tall lifters" IS the unique who), unique wording (drop in a unique power word: "disgustingly well spoken," 6M views, vs the reflexive "extremely"; "melt off body fat," 1.2M; "sage and golden advice"), or a unique angle, the strongest and most common in top performers: reach the same point from a direction nobody saw coming (baking soda as "the cheapest medicine they want you to think is just for baking"; opening with a statement you then call completely wrong; the protein smoothie as "nothing says I'm gonna be fuckable every day of the summer"). There is no formula, by definition: a formula everyone uses stops being unique. Source raw hooks from the content care package, then run the three moves.

**12. The shtick-talk smell test.**
Diagnosis: "lose 30 pounds in 60 days" sounds like shtick talk the moment it's said, because it's the pattern everyone runs.
Fix: full pipeline check before scripting: narrow problem chosen, solution passes Trinity of Value, and the presentation doesn't sound like everyone else. That's the checklist at the bottom of the call worksheet.

## Process sins (from Q&A)

**13. Skipping the post over fixing a known-bad video: doing it backwards.**
Diagnosis: fear of "the algorithm punishing a missed day" ships bad videos on schedule. There is no algorithmic penalty: Devin has gone ~40 days dark and returned to a million views. Content is winner-take-all; quality earns exponential rewards.
Fix: if you KNOW the video is bad and know the fixes, hold it a day and fix it. But never slide into perfectionist iteration in a vacuum: without the skill to know what incredible looks like, you need real-world feedback more than polish. Post to learn; hold only when the fix is concrete.

**14. Winging the script on camera.**
Diagnosis: Charles, eighteen months in, disliked how he looked on camera. Root cause: he never memorized the script, so delivery died.
Fix: memorize the lines, then deliver them your own way. Near-verbatim with natural delivery beats reading and beats improvising.

**15. Applying feedback verbatim across contexts.**
Diagnosis: a scriptwriter took "your face must be in the video" as law and applied it to POV-style content where faceless was the format.
Fix: transfer principles, not prescriptions. And expect a feedback trajectory: batch one ~30% usable, then 50%, then 70%. If it's not climbing, the feedback loop is broken.

**16. Molding yourself to a stolen idea.**
Diagnosis: chasing an inspiration into a format that isn't yours (going full run-and-gun videography because the source video did).
Fix: the Jeff Nippard frame: the idea molds to him, never the reverse. Steal the concept, keep your format.


## Finding narrow ideas: the Idea Tree
(Use whenever the entrepreneur asks for video ideas, a content calendar, or "what should I film.")

1. **Content quadrants first.** Name the 3-4 broad topic buckets this business makes videos about. Every video lives in one.
2. **Build the tree.** Pick a quadrant. Write its broadest problem at the top. Each step down makes the PROBLEM smaller, never the audience. ("How to exercise to build muscle" → "grow legs" → "best quad machine" → "best seat position for leg extensions." That last step was a 7.2M-view video, and nobody who cared about step one was cut along the way.)
3. **Two stop rules.** Stop when the next step would (a) cut out a chunk of ideal viewers (that's niching, not narrowing), or (b) pass the mascot's awareness (they'd need the thing explained before the video could help).
4. **Solve problems, not steps.** Each rung is its own complete video, never "part 2 of the big one."
5. **Vertical vs horizontal.** Down the tree = narrower. Sideways at the same depth = sibling videos, equally good. Know which move you're making.
6. **The tree is a content calendar.** One broad idea yields roughly nine narrow videos. When generating ideas, show the tree, mark the stop rung, and offer siblings.

**Multiply the work.** One tree = a content calendar (each rung and sibling is a video). One content idea = several hooks: the same teaching opens differently per awareness level and clarity mechanism, so gauntlet runners-up are future openers, not waste. Same script can be recorded more than once with different visual hooks.
