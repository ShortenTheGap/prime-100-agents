# Script Structure: The 5 Components

Every script is assembled from five interchangeable parts. Only the hook has a fixed position (always first). Label every sentence of every script with its component.

## The components

### 1. Hook (position: ALWAYS first; importance: #1)
The first seconds. Sole job: grab attention. If this fails, nothing else matters.
- Verbal hook: the spoken line ("I'm gonna save you 30 minutes of your next workout with 3 simple tips").
- Visual hook: the on-screen representation of the verbal hook (see hooks.md for the full three-type system).
- Anti-pattern: shtick talk, borrowing a hook that hit without knowing why it hit (scripting-sins.md, Sin 3). Before using any borrowed hook, name the reason it worked, then make it yours via unique presentation, unique wording, or unique angle (strongest).

### 2. Interest Peak (position: anywhere; the most flexible component)
Re-grabs attention wherever the viewer might get bored. A defibrillator for attention. Canonical definition (Interest Peak Mastery workshop): a strategically placed line that re-engages viewers so they keep watching. The hook only gets people to CONSIDER watching; the interest peak makes them stick.

**Two placements, with usage rules:**
- **Double Down** (immediately after the hook, locks the viewer in). NOT every video. The gate test: "Is this hook alone enough to get someone 100% bought in?" If yes, skip the double down; it costs value per second. Classic double-down case: the hook is desirable but nothing answers "why should I listen to you?"
- **Mid-Roll** (mid-value, reels them back in). Use whenever delivery is getting predictable and the mundane is setting in, typically between steps.

**The eight types (canonical; each works in either placement):**
1. **Call Out**: name the viewer by label or action ("8 out of 10 guys in the gym do this. Don't be one of them.")
2. **Hype Up**: raise the perceived significance of what's coming ("Now here's where the money's made.")
3. **Social Proof**: receipts that raise believability ("Coming from someone who went from broke to the top 1% of 24-year-olds.")
4. **Risk Reversal**: lower the perceived cost/risk of listening or doing ("Don't worry, this won't get you fired." / "This next step takes under a minute.")
5. **Consequences**: raise the stakes of skipping or botching it ("This is all for nothing if you screw up this last part.")
6. **Negative Assumption**: kill the wrong assumption they're carrying ("And no, you don't need an expensive camera.") **Gate before using: held + blocking + not better shown.** The mascot must actually hold the assumption, it must be blocking him at this moment, and visual disproof beats spoken disproof. Unfelt objections get planted by voicing them. Max one spoken objection-kill per script unless objections are verified from real audience data.
7. **Cost Narration**: what the knowledge cost YOU in time/money/effort ("I paid a mentor $5,000 to learn this.")
8. **Guided Question** (Devin's favorite, strongest as mid-roll): answer the hook's question, then immediately pose the next question the viewer was about to think ("So if it's not age, why is it harder to lose weight after your 20s?"). **Qualifying test: was the viewer's brain already forming this question?** A manufactured question that exists only to set up your next line fails, and its answer is usually cuttable information ("why aren't you using it? Because it's buried under ten thousand comments" = a question nobody asked, answered with a fact nobody needed).

**Retention-cliff diagnosis (use whenever the entrepreneur brings retention graphs):** two cliffs, two fixes. A steep drop RIGHT AFTER the hook means the hook wrote a promise the next line didn't cash: add a double down (or fix the hook's overpromise). A cliff in the MIDDLE means the video turned into a list and went mundane: add a mid-roll. The line always goes down; the job is killing cliffs, not flattening the line.

Taxonomy is a generation tool, not a classification exam: real lines often fit two types; pick the best fit and move on. Can repeat: strong scripts have used three interest peaks and zero promise. (Supersedes the old three-type list: "authority builder" = Social Proof.)

### 3. Value (importance: #2)
Does exactly one of two things: (a) makes the viewer aware of a problem, then solves it, or (b) solves a problem they already know they have. Must pass the Trinity gate (trinity-of-value.md) before the script is written.
Five traits of valuable video (the earlier checklist Devin re-quizzes): relevant (bigger problem = more perceived value), clear solution, viewer depictable, unique, worth $5 (the lottery test: if they'd paid $5 to land here, did they win?).
Warning: a great hook on weak value builds negative reinforcement. You trained the viewer to distrust your next video.

### 4. Promise (the most underrated component)
The viewer pays with attention now but collects the reward only after acting later. The promise is the receipt: proof the payoff is real, inserted right after (or inside) the value.
Example: "Going viral isn't luck. It's calculated. Don't believe me? My last 24 videos all got over 100K views."
Format per Commandment 6: definitive outcome, specific floor, zero qualifiers.

### 5. CTA (position: usually last; see cta-system.md)
Ask the viewer to do something. Conversion depends on: how good the value was, how specific the ask, how much they like you. Not every video gets one; see the loop/snap alternatives.

## Assembly rules
- Order is flexible; only the hook is locked.
- Use 3, 4, or 5 components as the video demands. Components can repeat.
- The structure serves two masters: the goal of the video, and keeping the value entertaining.

## Canonical labeled examples

**Devin's Mixkit video (all five, canonical order):**
- Hook: "Here's a secret website that gives you unlimited stock footage, stock music, and sound effects."
- Interest peak: "Oh, and did I mention it's free?"
- Value: Go to mixkit.co, thousands of free resources.
- Promise: "Just search for whatever you're looking for and I promise it'll have it."
- CTA: "But as always, follow for more value."

**Toozer's video (structure bending to the content: 3 interest peaks, no promise):**
- Hook: "I haven't seen anybody talk about this business model yet. Super untapped way to make minimum $10K/month."
- Interest peak: "Takes under a minute to set up, costs less than lunch."
- Value: build the AI tool...
- Interest peak: "My favorite free marketing method. I've grown businesses to $20K/day with this."
- Value: distribution steps...
- Interest peak: "This is the best part. Here's where the money's made."
- Value: retargeting ads...
- CTA: "Free tutorial on the ads. Comment FB and I'll DM it."

## Ordering the value itself (Commandment 8)
- Sequenced information → linear storytelling: announce type and count early ("in three steps"), consistent markers, transitions at every hand-off.
- Unsequenced tips → sandwich: best tip FIRST (commits the viewer), second-best LAST (peak-end memory drives shares and CTA conversion), the rest best-to-worst in between.
