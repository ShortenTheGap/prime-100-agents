# Email types and voice

Read this before writing any email in a runway. It describes what each email type does and the beats it needs. It contains no example copy on purpose: an example is a voice, and the only voice that belongs in a member's runway is the member's.

## Rule zero: the owner's voice, the owner's world

Every email is written in the register of the owner's three voice samples, using metaphors from the owner's own trade, with the owner's own clients as proof. The examples used in Prime workshops, and any other business's emails the owner admires, are illustrations of the method. Nothing from them goes into a member's emails: no lines, no subject lines, no metaphors, no nicknames, no signature tags, no client names, no programs, no numbers. If a phrase in a draft could be traced back to someone else's email, cut it and write the owner's version.

The same type flexes by register. A "character" email from a plastic surgeon is a note about a long day in theatre and a full book through spring. From a lighting installer it's a photo of a crew on a roof at 6am. From a PhD fitness coach it's what a study got wrong. From a CPA it's the client who called at 4pm on the filing deadline. Same job, four different people. The skill's job is to find the owner's version, not the generic one.

## Voice fingerprint

Before writing, build this from the owner's samples and confirm it in one line. Store it in the runway file under Brief.

- Register: 1 (formal, professional, no jokes) to 5 (casual, personal, jokes fine). Most trades and professions sit at 2 or 3. Don't drift up.
- Humor: none / dry / open. Default none until the samples show otherwise.
- Profanity: none unless the owner's samples use it. Never introduce it.
- Sentence and paragraph length: match the samples. Short one-line paragraphs suit a casual sender; a professional who writes in full paragraphs keeps writing in full paragraphs.
- Pronouns: I or we. A solo owner says I. A practice or a company with a team usually says we.
- Sign-off: exactly how the owner signs. No invented nicknames or taglines.
- House terms: the words the owner already uses for their method, their clients, their service.
- Never-says: three to five words or moves the owner would never make (slang, hype, swearing, exclamation marks, emoji, "guys"). Check every draft against this list.

Buyer language comes from the Living Avatar and Quote Bank. Sender language comes from the samples. Don't mix them up: the reader's words go in the problem and the belief; the owner's words go in the telling.

## Universal rules

- One belief per email. One asset per email. One link. The P.S. restates that link and nothing else.
- Name the belief in a phrase the owner would say, using an image from their own field. The name is what the reader remembers; the email argues for it.
- Subject lines earn the open without lying. Casual registers can use surprise; professional registers use specificity. A subject that promises something the email doesn't deliver is a blocker.
- Open in the middle of something: a moment, a number, a question the reader is already asking. No "I hope this finds you well," no recap of the last email.
- Proof is specific or it's cut: who (with permission), where they started, what changed, over how long. "Great results" is not proof.
- Villains are named fairly. The common approach, the industry default, the advice everyone repeats. Not a competitor by name, and never a straw man.
- Don't assume the reader consumed the last email.
- Belief Shaping emails never pitch. The asks are Day 1, Day 2, the Spear, and the Close.

## Asset blocks

Anchor assets are the owner's best long pieces: a 20 to 40 minute video, a webinar replay, a workshop recording, a podcast episode, a tool. Each anchor supports three to five emails that point at the same link from different angles. That's how a member with four good pieces gets fifteen emails without recording anything new.

A block, in the usual order: direct pointer (here's the thing, why it matters, go), then a belief break, then a client story or receipts, then an objection killer or a useful tool. Not every block needs all five. A thin anchor gets two emails. Blocks run in MOAT belief order.

## The 12 types

For each: the job, the beats, what it needs from the vault, and the trap.

**1. Resource delivery (Day 1).** Job: hand over what they asked for and open a reply route. Beats: here's the thing, what it helps you do, the first step, "if you'd like help with [outcome], reply and tell me what you're working on." Optional: a one-line self-segmentation ("which of these is closest to your situation? reply with a, b, or c"). Needs: the entry resource link. Trap: teaching or pitching in the delivery email.

**2. Personal Note (Day 2).** Job: get a reply. Beats: I sent you [resource] yesterday, one easy question, sign-off. Four lines. Needs: the owner's sign-off. Trap: two questions, or a question that needs a paragraph to answer.

**3. Direct pointer.** Job: open a block by sending the reader to the anchor. Beats: what the owner made and who it's for, why it matters to this reader now, what they'll get from it, the link. Needs: an inspected anchor with a current CTA. Trap: describing the asset instead of selling the reason to consume it.

**4. Origin story.** Job: install the block's belief through the owner's own history. Beats: the moment things were wrong, the specific cost, the decision, what changed, the belief it left behind, the link. Needs: a real story the owner told you. Trap: inventing detail. If the owner didn't say it, it isn't in the email.

**5. Belief break.** Job: replace a common belief with the owner's. Beats: state the old belief plainly and fairly, why it fails (with a reason, not a sneer), the new belief named in the owner's image, one example, the asset that goes deeper. Needs: a MOAT belief and something in the vault that argues it. Trap: a tips list. Tips are what to know; this email is what to want.

**6. Process explainer.** Job: show how the owner's method works so the reader can picture it. Beats: the step, what happens, why it matters, what it moves the client toward, the asset that shows it. Needs: the owner's process pillars. Trap: jargon the buyer would have to learn before they could value the step.

**7. Client story.** Job: prove the belief works for someone like the reader. Beats: who they were when they arrived (in terms the reader recognizes), the specific problem, what the owner changed, the result with a number and a period, one line on what it meant to them, the link. Needs: reuse permission and the actual figures. Trap: a testimonial quote standing in for a story.

**8. Receipts.** Job: show raw evidence from a live run. Beats: what was done, the numbers as they are, why it worked, the tool or method behind it, the link. Needs: a screenshot or figures the owner can stand behind, labeled as estimates where they are. Trap: rounding up.

**9. Useful tool or exercise.** Job: give a win inside the email. Beats: the problem in one line, the exercise (complete, doable in minutes), what the result tells them, the asset that extends it. Needs: a working tool or an exercise the owner actually uses (a scorecard, a checklist, a short diagnostic). Trap: an exercise that requires the owner's help to finish.

**10. Thought experiment.** Job: make the reader feel a gap they hadn't noticed. Beats: set up an explicit hypothetical, put the reader in it, the question it forces, what the answer reveals, the asset. Needs: nothing from the vault beyond the belief; keep the scenario in the owner's field. Trap: a scenario so odd it reads as a gimmick for a formal sender.

**11. Objection killer.** Job: take one hesitation head-on. Beats: the objection in the reader's words (from the Quote Bank), why it's reasonable, the case or fact that answers it, the link. Needs: a MOAT false belief and an answer with evidence. Trap: arguing with the reader instead of answering them.

**12. Character.** Job: show who the owner is and that demand is real, with no pitch. Beats: a moment from the owner's week, what it says about how they work, optionally that the calendar or the book is full, sign-off. No link, or a link to nothing sellable. Needs: a real moment the owner shared and true demand facts. Trap: bragging, or manufacturing "sold out."

Then the asks, which have their own fixed patterns in SKILL.md: the Spear, and the Close (invitation, details, three-send final day).

## Register flex: one beat, two owners

These two fragments exist only to show how the same beat changes with register. Both businesses are invented. Do not reuse a phrase from either; write the owner's version.

The beat: a belief break's "state the old belief fairly, then why it fails."

A physiotherapy clinic, register 2, full paragraphs, we:

> Most people who come to us for a shoulder have already been told to rest it. That advice isn't wrong, and for the first week it's usually right. The trouble starts in week three, when rest has become the plan. A shoulder that's been protected for a month has learned to be protected, and undoing that takes longer than the injury did.

A roofing company, register 4, short lines, I:

> Everyone tells you to get three quotes.
> Fine. Get three.
> But here's what nobody tells you: two of them are guessing. They priced it from the driveway.
> The one who went up on the roof is the only number that means anything.

Same beat. Different sentence length, different pronoun, different image, both from inside the owner's trade. That's the whole skill.

## The borrowed-voice check

Run on every draft before review. Any hit is a revision.

- A phrase, subject line, metaphor, or joke that exists in a Prime workshop deck or in another business's emails.
- A nickname or tagline in the signature the owner doesn't use.
- Slang, profanity, or humor the samples don't contain.
- A client, program, tool, number, or story that belongs to Prime or any other business.
- A universal claim about buying behavior (minutes consumed, conversion lifts) presented as fact.
- Scarcity, a deadline, capacity, or a discount the owner didn't state.
- Short punchy one-liners in a runway for a sender whose samples write in paragraphs, or the reverse.
- "We" in a solo owner's voice, or "I" in a practice that speaks as a team.
- Any word on the owner's never-says list.
